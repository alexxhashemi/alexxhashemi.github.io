Research

My research looks at how speech recognition fails people with speech impairments, and at how those failures shape decisions about hiring, services and access.

I approach it from both sides: measuring the failures with software testing methods, and asking people with speech impairments what counts as a failure in the first place.

Auditing Speech Recognition for Atypical Speech

Ongoing project · York University, Social and Technological Systems (SaTS) Lab · Jan 2026 to present

Automatic speech recognition now sits in the path of hiring, benefits and healthcare access, where one mis-transcribed answer can lower an interview score or send a call to the wrong queue. This project asks how those failures can be found and what they cost.

It begins with a word-error-rate audit of Whisper and Google Cloud Speech-to-Text on two clinical datasets of dysarthric speech, TORGO and UA-Speech, analyzed separately because their severity scales differ. This is the basis of my Major Research Paper at York.

The project is supported by my work as a research assistant in York's SaTS Lab on When People Talk, Listen Completely, which looks at communication access and employment barriers for people with speech impairment.

Where errors become decisions

A speech recognition error can look harmless. If someone says "hi, i'm alex" and the system writes "hi, i'm a lax", that is just a typo on a screen. In a school, an employer, or a public agency, the same error can become a missed accommodation, a failed job screening, or a person marked as unclear.
